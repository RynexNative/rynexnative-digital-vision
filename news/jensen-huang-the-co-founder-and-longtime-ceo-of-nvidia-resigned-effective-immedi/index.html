Jensen Huang, the co-founder and longtime CEO of Nvidia, resigned effective immediately on November 12, 2026, following a multi-layered scandal involving extramarital affairs and U.S. national security concerns over Chinese espionage. His sudden departure abruptly ends a 33-year tenure during which he built the chipmaker into the dominant powerhouse of the artificial intelligence boom, peaking at a $5 trillion valuation just weeks earlier.

Timeline of the Scandal

• Initial Intelligence Reports (Late October 2026). Reports emerged in The New York Times revealing that U.S. intelligence officials suspected Jensen Huang had been targeted by a Chinese intelligence operation. At the time, Nvidia dismissed the concerns, stating there was nothing unusual about his business interactions.
• The Espionage and Megaspeed Connection (November 6, 2026). Further investigative reporting from The New York Times and Financial Times identified the woman at the center of the intelligence probe as Alice Huang (also reported as Alice Wong), a senior executive at Megaspeed International. U.S. officials allege she is a Chinese intelligence asset who used her romantic relationship with Huang to acquire at least $7.5 billion worth of restricted Nvidia AI chips for China via the Singapore-based cloud company, bypassing U.S. export controls.
• Public Admission and Board Investigation (November 8, 2026). Facing mounting pressure, Huang sent a letter to Nvidia staff admitting he had been unfaithful to his wife of over three decades, Lori, and apologized to his family and employees. Nvidia's Board of Directors initially stood behind him while hiring the prominent law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to conduct an independent investigation into whether national security or corporate secrets had been compromised.
• Internal Workplace Affairs Exposed (November 11, 2026). The crisis reached a breaking point when The New York Times and Reuters revealed that Huang had engaged in separate extramarital affairs with employees inside Nvidia. One of the women was identified as Kimberly Powell, a senior Nvidia executive running the company's healthcare division who reported directly to Huang.
• Resignation and Ouster (November 12, 2026). With internal governance rules violated by relationships with direct reports and investor confidence crumbling, the board met to discuss his removal. Shortly afterward, Nvidia officially announced that Huang had resigned as President, CEO, and member of the Board of Directors.

Leadership Transition and Immediate Fallout

• Interim Leadership. Nvidia's board appointed longtime Executive Vice President and Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress as Interim CEO while launching a search for a permanent successor among both internal and external candidates. Longtime board member Harvey C. Jones was simultaneously named Nvidia's first-ever Chair of the Board.

The fallout from Jensen Huang’s resignation has split into two major storylines: a high-stakes race to replace a founder who left no clear heir, and a deepening geopolitical crisis in Washington and Southeast Asia over how restricted AI chips reached China.

1. The Search for Nvidia's Next CEO

Finding a permanent successor for Huang is uniquely difficult because he maintained an unusually flat organizational structure with roughly 60 executives reporting directly to him and no designated "number two". While CFO Colette Kress serves as interim CEO, Nvidia’s board has hired an outside search firm to evaluate both internal and external contenders.
• Rene Haas (CEO of Arm Holdings). According to the Financial Times, Nvidia’s board has already approached Haas. He spent seven years as an executive at Nvidia more than a decade ago, maintains close ties with the board, and leads Arm—whose chip architecture powers Nvidia's Grace and Vera CPUs.
• Ian Buck (Nvidia VP of Hyperscale and HPC). Considered the top internal technical candidate, Buck runs Nvidia’s data center business and invented CUDA, the foundational software platform that locked developers into Nvidia’s ecosystem. However, analysts note he lacks experience running a massive publicly traded company.
• Lisa Su (CEO of AMD). Frequently cited by Wall Street analysts as a dream external pick, Su is widely credited with engineering AMD's turnaround into a major semiconductor powerhouse, though poaching her from Nvidia’s primary rival would be a massive corporate coup.
• Other External Tech Leaders. Names circulating in executive recruitment and political circles include Apple hardware chief Johny Srouji, AWS CEO Matt Garman, Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra, and former Apple CEO Tim Cook, whom President Donald Trump publicly suggested for the role.

2. The Megaspeed Loophole and Geopolitical Fallout

The scandal has exposed major weaknesses in U.S. efforts to keep advanced artificial intelligence hardware out of China, triggering political infighting in Washington and crackdowns in Southeast Asia.
• How Megaspeed Operated. Spun off from a Chinese gaming company, Singapore-based Megaspeed International rapidly grew into Nvidia’s largest Southeast Asian customer, purchasing over $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia AI equipment between 2024 and 2026. Operating as a "neocloud" provider, Megaspeed built data centers in Malaysia and Singapore to legally rent computing power to Chinese tech giants like Alibaba—a major loophole in U.S. export rules. U.S. investigators also suspect the firm physically smuggled restricted chips across the border into China.
• Singapore's Crackdown. On November 12, Singapore’s Ministry of Home Affairs announced it had permanently barred Alice Huang (born Huang Le) from re-entering the country and issued her a stern warning for giving false statements to police to downplay her leadership role at Megaspeed. Singaporean authorities noted they had impounded her passport in 2025 but received "limited information or assistance" from U.S. officials when they requested help investigating the company.
• Infighting in the Trump Administration. The scandal has sparked public blame inside Washington. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC that multiple agencies had pushed for months to blacklist Megaspeed, blaming Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick for delaying the move. Reports from The New York Times indicate the Commerce Department had previously halted a chip inquiry into Megaspeed following direct intervention from Nvidia.
• Congressional Subpoenas. Both the House and the Senate Banking Committee—chaired by Republican Senator Tim Scott—have summoned Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December regarding the national security lapses, warning of formal subpoenas if they do not appear. Meanwhile, China's Foreign Ministry has warned Washington against using the allegations as a pretext to impose stricter export controls on AI chips.

1. How Nvidia Stock and the AI Market Are Reacting

• Sharp Selloff and Retail Dip-Buying. When news of Huang’s resignation broke on November 12, Nvidia shares plunged roughly 14% in early trading before retail dip-buyers stepped in, narrowing the daily loss to 4.34%. Over the week since the scandal first erupted, Nvidia has lost more than $300 billion in market value, pushing its valuation below $4 trillion.
• Wall Street Succession Fears. Analysts warn that Nvidia faces an "uninvestable" period of uncertainty because Huang had no clear successor and personally managed key government and customer relationships. Investment firm Bernstein responded by slashing its price target on Nvidia in half from $400 to $200.
• Ripple Effects Across the AI Ecosystem. Because Nvidia finances many of the startups and "neocloud" data centers that buy its chips—a practice known as circular financing—its turmoil dragged down partners like CoreWeave (-13.6%), Nebius (-20%), Micron (-5.8%), and Taiwan Semiconductor (-2.3%).
• Gains for Rivals. Competitors are seeing investor inflows on bets that customers will diversify their chip supply. Rivals AMD and Broadcom rose on Thursday following the resignation, while Chinese domestic chipmakers like Cambricon Technologies and MetaX surged over 8% in Shanghai on expectations of stronger local demand.

2. The U.S. Export Loophole and Southeast Asia Cloud Rentals

• How the Loophole Works. Under current U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) rules, Nvidia is banned from physically shipping its top-tier AI processors (such as H100, H200, and Blackwell chips) to China. However, U.S. export law regulates physical goods rather than remote cloud computing. This allowed Singapore- and Malaysia-based cloud operators like Megaspeed to legally buy billions of dollars in Nvidia hardware and rent out remote server access to Chinese tech giants like Alibaba.
• Physical Smuggling Probes. Alongside legal cloud rentals, U.S. prosecutors and Bloomberg investigations uncovered evidence that entities linked to Alice Huang physically diverted restricted Nvidia servers into mainland China, including facilities near Shanghai.
• Closing the Gap with the RASA Bill. The scandal has created overwhelming bipartisan momentum in Washington to pass the Remote Access Security Act (RASA). Already approved by the House of Representatives in a 369–22 vote earlier in 2026, the bill awaits Senate action and would give the U.S. Commerce Department explicit authority to block Chinese firms from remotely accessing restricted American chips via foreign data centers.
• Scramble for Compute Power. Fearing an imminent U.S. ban on offshore rentals and new Commerce Department restrictions targeting Malaysia and Singapore, Chinese AI labs are rushing to lock in long-term server leases across Southeast Asia before the loophole shuts.
